2Days 1 to 3

The CAD for CAM content is spread across the three days, four to six hours in total. It's taught alongside the CAM rather than as its own block. It covers the modelling a CAM programmer needs to do for themselves when the Onshape geometry isn't quite what the programming process needs, plus how to keep the whole thing organised once designs start changing underneath your programs.

Day 1: How Fusion works and your first program

All six of you.

We start with how Fusion holds data, how the Design and Manufacture workspaces relate to each other, and how I'd structure your projects so the CAM can optionally live in its own document and reference the design. Then how Onshape geometry comes across, the conventions that keep it tidy, and what happens when the design changes after you've already programmed it. Getting this bit right on day one saves a lot of pain later.

The afternoon is CAM from the ground up. Setups, work coordinate systems and stock, machine configuration for the Datron, then tools, holders and tool libraries, which we'll set up properly from the start. From there we'll program a simple part end to end: facing, contouring, pocketing and drilling, simulate it and post it, so everyone has seen the full workflow before we go any deeper.

Day 2: 2.5-axis and 3-axis milling

The three programmers all day, the other three for the first hour.

2.5-axis in depth. Adaptive clearing and why it suits a machine like the Datron, contour and pocket with the heights, passes and linking settings, pocket recognition and chain selection hygiene, so toolpaths can survive design changes, bore milling, drilling cycles, tapping and chamfering. We'll also go through toolpath ordering, rest machining, and the tooltips and expressions, which are a great way to build understanding of the software once you realise they're there.

Then 3-axis. 3D adaptive roughing, the finishing strategies and which one suits which sort of surface, containment and boundaries, stock to leave, and rest machining between operations. The CAD for CAM here is defeaturing, capping holes with surfaces and splitting faces to drive toolpath boundaries. You'll need these skills as parts are designed to represent their final state, and typically don't contain geometry for the manufacturing process itself.

Day 3: Positional 4-axis and workholding

Same split as day two: three programmers all day, three others for the first hour.

A rotary changes how you think about a setup, so we'll spend the morning on tool orientation, one work coordinate system versus several, indexing between faces, and laying out operations so the part comes off the machine finished. All of it simulated with stock, so you can see what the rotary is going to do before it does it.

Workholding gets its own block in the afternoon. Modelling your fixtures and vices as components, positioning stock properly, and building setups that reflect what's actually bolted to the machine.

We finish day three with a full part, start to finish, on one of my datasets: Onshape geometry in, setups, 2.5 and 3-axis operations, a rotary setup, simulate, post.

If there are parts you're not sure how to approach, those are the ones to send.
